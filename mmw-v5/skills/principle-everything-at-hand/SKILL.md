# Everything at Hand

On any page or diagram for a person, put everything needed to understand a place where the reader's eyes stop at that place. Count each "remember here, look elsewhere, come back" as a defect.

**Why:** Working memory is small, and the reader forgets the first place while looking elsewhere.

**Pattern:**

- Put explanations beside what they explain. Give every diagram its own legend covering every mark it uses, even when another diagram repeats the notation. Explain an unfamiliar term once beside its use in each diagram. Put fields, questions, and tradeoffs beside their screen or step, so the reader can understand that part in one view.
- Mark questions, changes, and decisions at their exact positions in the diagram. Pair each numbered mark with the same number in an adjacent note, and keep the item there alone, so the reader can find what the note concerns.
- Let pictures carry the content. Draw flows, structures, routes, who reads or writes what, and comparisons. Use tables for unrelated parallel entries. Keep box labels short. Words supply what pictures cannot: the question answered, the part to inspect, and the conclusion. Remove sentences that repeat the picture, and redraw a picture that needs a paragraph to be understood. Cite source detail rather than retelling it. This keeps paragraphs from replacing the thing the reader needs to see.
- Keep one subject on one canvas, with the whole in a large diagram and details nested inside or drawn directly beside it. Draw each component a step calls as its own element, with shape and colour distinguishing kinds. State omissions on the diagram so an apparently complete picture can be trusted. Revise the existing page and diagram when the design changes. Put before and after, or current and proposed, side by side at the same scale so comparison needs no remembered image.
- Show only the current design, per **principle-files-describe-the-present**, so the page gives the reader one design to judge.
- Lead the page with the conclusion, totals, and decisions needed from the reader, so the reader knows what to inspect and answer.

Use **principle-anchor-every-reference** for names.

**The test:** Starting at any mark on the page, can the reader say what it is and why it is there without scrolling, turning a page, or remembering the chat?

**Stop:** A fact that fits in one sentence gets that sentence. A drawing earns its space by explaining more than the sentence would.

Distinct from **principle-write-for-where-it-is-read**, which decides who reads and what they can see. This principle governs whether everything needed while reading a page is in view.
